### Zero-Entry Accounting Age (ZEAA) – Tribes (Pre 10,000 BCE – 3,500 BCE)

Humans lived in small bands of 20-50 people during this period until the Neolithic Revolution (~10,000 BCE), when agriculture enabled larger communities. Afterwards, early farming villages formed, but the total population didn’t scale much past _Dunbars number (~150)_ during this epoch—just small bands and tribes.

Identity was held in place entirely in the minds of the members of the tribe (reputation). **Reputation** **was everything** (it still is today but fortunately it’s not held in the malleable minds of our neighbors). Life, death, and roles depended entirely on how those 20-150 malleable minds felt about any individual during any given moment.

There was no way to record any state, and no way to survive alone as an individual. Without your identity, which exists in the minds of your tribal members—you’re ☠️☠️. That’s your ancient passport, it’s your reputation. Except it didn’t interoperate much, other tribes were mostly hostile and had no reputation for you = no identity = ☠️☠️.

#### Roles

**Identity was more fluid**. Roles could change rapidly due to circumstances and were made or changed using <u>rituals</u>.

There was no massive State holding you in position or trying to tax and identify you, just 20-150 people bands who communally arrived at a fluid consensus.

Identity shifts were centered around rituals: the ritual of birth, the ritual of becoming a man, the ritual of becoming a mother, the ritual of becoming a hunter, the ritual of becoming an elder, the ritual of dying. The rituals of nightly talks and confessions around a fire could shift fluid roles.

The legacies of these rituals weren’t recorded. Everything here is suited for impermanence[3](#fn:3). There was no paperwork or documents holding identities in place.

> Identities were vivid flames, not frozen files.

The specialization of roles was fluid, it wasn’t like they were training their whole lives or bloodlines to become scribes, lawyers, or doctors. There just wasn’t that much specialization or hierarchy.

Individuals had to fit their “role” into the tribal State’s shared reality of social cohesion (a small range), their only freedom to differentiate was inside that tiny range. Lots of death outside that range.

Their whole concept of lasting identity probably wasn’t as deep as ours. They probably didn’t think about who they were so much, because it constantly changed.

An individuals past and history could easily be erased and adjusted. All they had to do was convince the malleable minds of the members of their tribe.

Roles were fully ethnically based, your tribe _<u>was</u>_ your ethnicity.

#### Privacy

While privacy from the tribe was rare; privacy over time was common. Whatever an individual did, could easily be wiped from the record, _because there was no record_. No CCTV cameras, no credit scores…You were tracked by grandmothers. _It was basically like Italy._

#### Individualism

Individualism was suppressed. Identity was communal property. Isolation was reduced in small bands, and the social pressure to conform was amplified.

The shared reality heavily controlled identity, limiting individualism and specialization to only the identities the tribe _required_. The tribal State’s small margin for bare survival didn’t allow it to invest in the R&D of individualism—anyone standing out as too eccentric faced ostracism.

The social cohesion (reputation system) was heavily influenced by the tribal elders, who had outsized control and influence over the consensus of the shared reality.

This bred humility, but limited self-actualization. Eccentrics were tolerated if useful, but outliers risked exile.

There was less specialization, less distinction between roles. Making people more or less the same.

#### Coordination

Tribes couldn’t coordinate well during the ZEA age. They were unable to create specialized individualistic individuals (who would facilitate greater coordination) or record state to help them coordinate. As a result, tribal societies during the ZEA age couldn’t scale.

#### Agency

To change their identity, individuals had to convince and act on the malleable minds of the members of their tribe. There was nothing else holding in place the fluidity of roles.

#### Summary & Limitations

Knowledge couldn’t spread (the death of “elder” knowledge holders meant setting the entire tribe back), information couldn’t spread (it kept mutating), societies couldn’t scale (empires only emerged in the SEA age), identities couldn’t specialize or become individualistic (the tribe didn’t have enough R&D resources and it would ruin social cohesion).

Early societies had lots of identity limitations. Identity was constrained to a small box (fluid but in a really narrow range). Property rights weren’t able to be recorded, and neither was language. People were generally dumb and they couldn’t communicate or coordinate well. Individuals couldn’t specialize or record roles for long enough to make them last.

### Single-Entry Accounting Age (SEAA) – Classical States (3500 BCE–476 CE)

Written records enabled the accumulation of knowledge, bureaucratic control, and cultural preservation. This fundamentally altered identity from fluid collective orality…to documented hierarchical forms. It transformed identity into something archivable and verifiable, shifting the emphasis from memory to inscribed records.

Hierarchies emerged. Classical States imposed documented identities for citizenship records (censuses), taxes, and military conscription. This boosted coordination amongst elites while eroding power, privacy, and tribal bonds of the vast majority— who couldn’t alter the recorded state.

Knowledge became separated from identities themselves for the first time. During the zero-entry accounting age, “elder loss” meant losing all the knowledge stored within that person. Identity was treated as synonymous with knowledge. It was only once records emerged that identity and knowledge become separated.

Ownership of identity was still intimately tied to reputation and the opinion of the close ones around you, but it was also inscribed by the scribes and in a macro sense communicated up the empire to the pharaohs and their representatives as rough macro mappings. Identity could now scale to empires through records.

#### Roles

Fluidity decreased in favor of specialization. Identity became role-based and codified (e.g., "citizen", “pharaoh”, or "slave"), enhancing efficiency but stifling improvisation.

Society acquired and recorded specialized roles, ranks, and skills (such as “tax collector”, “scribe”, or “legionary”) but it was not great or meritocratic in the ways it prescribed individuals those roles. It’s talent-finding mechanisms were rusty until the double-entry accounting age.

Identity became more engrained in people. It became less ephemeral and more inscribed, this enabled enduring legacies and changed identities to consider their legacy.

Roles were still heavily ethnically based, but interoperable: Greek city-states like Athens often defined citizenship through descent, emphasizing Athenian lineage. Foreigners (metics) were excluded from full political rights, and myths of common ancestry reinforced group cohesion. Identity was linked to specific ethnic markers.

#### Individualism

Individuals at the top retained a deep sense of individuality and uniqueness while those who couldn't store or record the state became commoditized as peasants and slaves. Slavery was documented, commodifying people who couldn't be differentiated with a specialized unique role. You could say the elites “commoditized their compliments”.

Assets were able to be inscribed for the first time and identities became able to accumulate lasting property. The SEA enabled rough economic tracking, tying personal worth to recorded assets.

#### Privacy

Privacy decreased significantly; records like tax ledgers and censuses (e.g., Roman census every 5 years) created state surveillance, tracking names, property, and debts. No more "forgetting" mistakes—debts or crimes were permanent.

Still, this tracking wasn’t very precise.

#### Coordination

Coordination vastly improved; bureaucracies managed large-scale projects (e.g., pyramids, aqueducts) via written orders and accounts. Single-entry ledgers tracked resources efficiently, enabling armies of 100,000+ (e.g., Persian or Roman).

Empires could coordinate massive endeavors with more specialized individualistic roles at the top and many commoditized undifferentiated roles (peasants and slaves) at the middle and bottom of the pyramid.

This enabled society to build large lumbering empires but they ultimately, weren’t too coordinated. They could use rough macro coordination without micro details.  
  
They could use rough macro coordination from the top, but had trouble coordinating horizontally or coordinating precise micro details.

#### Agency

To change identity became more difficult, as it now required changing this hierarchical structure and changing the recordings of it.

#### Summary & Limitations

During the SEAA massive lumbering hierarchical empire structure formed. They swallowed up tribes as empires swelled in size—absorbing tribes into their shared reality. Society became able to manage large scale projects and bureocracies emerged.

Identity became more rigid and specialized. Formal roles were assigned and difficult to alter, knowledge was separated from the source individual, property records emerged.

The SEAA was limited by its inability to optimize the specialized roles of individuals in a non-ascribed optimized ways, its inability to scale identities for the vast majority (99%) globally effectively (other than vertical integration), and its inability to handle individualism (lumbering empires couldn’t handle much differentiation from the POV of the ruling king or pharaoh until the DEAA).
